## Reader context

**Awareness stage:** Barely Problem-Aware. They clicked out of curiosity from a cold email they didn't ask for. The page has roughly eight seconds to justify the click.

**Sophistication stage:** Stage 1 for the real claim. "Get a website" is worn out, but "change it yourself by typing what you want" is genuinely new to this audience. Say the plain thing plainly.

**Structural consequence:** The video goes first — not below the fold, not after a preamble. It's the reason they clicked and it's the strongest proof available (top of the proof ladder, above testimonials). Everything else on the page answers questions the video creates.

**Belief chain the page walks:**
> a website matters → *changing it yourself* is the thing that matters → this actually works → it'll work for me → $950 is fair → email him

Weakest link is "does it actually work?" — that's what the video carries.

## SECTION 2 — Why one page

## Why one page is the right place to start

Most businesses I talk to have never had a website. Not because they didn't want one — it just kept getting pushed down the list.

And when they do look into it, they get quoted for something big. Multiple pages, a blog, a booking system, a photo gallery. Fair enough if you know you need all that. But if you've never had a site at all, it's a lot of money and a lot of decisions about something you can't picture yet.

Here's what actually happens when someone finds you online. They want to know three things:

**Who you are. What you do. How to get hold of you.**

That's it. That's the whole job for most businesses, and one page does it properly.

Right now, someone finds you on Google Maps, goes looking for more, and finds nothing. A single page fixes that completely. Not partly — completely.

And here's the thing about websites: they're never finished. Every good one gets added to over time. Prices change, you take on new work, you get a photo worth showing off. The difference is you'll be making those calls once your site is live and you can see it — instead of guessing what you might need before you've had one at all.

So start simple. Get online. See what people actually ask you about, what you find yourself wanting to add.

Then add it yourself, whenever you like.

## SECTION 4 — What goes on the page

## What actually goes on your page

A one-pager isn't a stripped-back website. It's everything that matters, in one place, in the right order:

- **Your business name and what you do** — clear in the first three seconds
- **Your phone number at the top** — tappable, so anyone on a mobile can get you without hunting for it
- **What you actually do** — your services listed out, so someone scanning can spot the thing they came for
- **Who you work with, and where** — the areas you cover or the sort of clients you take on, so nobody's left guessing whether you're for them
- **Who you are** — a few lines about the business, and your face if you're happy to have it there. People like knowing who they're dealing with.
- **Why you're worth trusting** — how long you've been going, qualifications, memberships, insurance. Whatever's true for you.
- **Photos of your work** — if you've got them. For some businesses this is the whole pitch. For others it's not relevant at all.
- **Your Google reviews** — if you've got good ones, they go on the page. You've already earned them.
- **When you're available** — hours, and anything worth flagging like after-hours or weekend work
- **How to get in touch** — phone, email, and a form if you want one

Most of that already exists on your Google listing. So "send me your details" is usually a smaller job than it sounds.

# NOTES & OPEN ITEMS

## Flags on the copy as written

**The "Who's doing this" section is thin and doing heavy lifting.** With zero testimonials, a stranger is being asked for $950 on the strength of a video and one link. That section is where a cold reader decides whether Craig is real.

- Add a photo — a real one, not a headshot
- Name the second site once it launches
- Local proof matters disproportionately in a regional market

**The free mock-up offer in the final CTA is the strongest close available, and it costs nothing.** If a rough mock-up of *their* business can genuinely be turned around in an hour, that demonstrates the speed claim rather than asserting it. Worth building a system for.

**Line placement to watch:** "Pay once. Never pay for a change again." is the best line on the page and it's currently buried in Section 3. If the page underperforms, moving it into the hero is the first thing to try.

## Variants to test

**Hero opening.** As written, the video leads. The alternative opens on the price comparison — *"You can get a website around here for $500. Here's why this one's $950."* More confrontational; suits readers already burnt by a web developer.

**Video framing lines.** Alternatives to "A real website, changed in real time. Nothing sped up.":
- Ninety seconds. This is what changing your website looks like.
- Sounds too easy. Here's it happening in real time.
- Watch someone change their opening hours in about a minute.

- A website. Not a relationship with a web developer.

## Repeatable phrase for the mechanism

Chosen direction: **"You just type what you want."**

It survives being repeated by someone else because it isn't a phrase — it's just how a person would describe it. Use identical phrasing across page, email and in person; repetition is what makes a phrase repeatable.

Rejected: "a website you can talk to" (invites the assumption it's voice-controlled, which it isn't).

## Production note on the demo video

Have the person on screen be someone who obviously isn't a developer. If it looks like a tech person doing tech things, the video proves the wrong thing. It should look like it could be them.

## Single page only — multi-page tier dropped

A $1,900 multi-page tier was proposed and then cut. Reasoning:

The skill's default output is ~18 pages — homepage, 5 service pages, 5-12 location pages, about, contact, service area, legals, 404. That structure exists to win local SEO in **dense US metro markets**, where you're fighting for "{service} in {suburb}" against a dozen competitors.

**Coffs Harbour doesn't work that way.** Not enough suburbs worth separate pages, not enough competitive density to need them. Building 18 pages for a business that has never had one solves a problem they don't have.

So: one page, one price. The free second page within 90 days covers the genuine cases.

Reconsider if: the market shifts, or a prospect turns up who genuinely needs local SEO depth (multi-suburb service area, competitive category). That's a custom quote, not a tier.

## What to keep from the skill even on a single page

The skill is disciplined about things the buyer never sees, and that discipline is what separates a $950 page from a $500 one:

- Unique title + meta description, canonical tag
- JSON-LD LocalBusiness schema
- Sitemap + robots.txt
- Security headers (`_headers` file)
- Image compression — 1400-1600px wide, JPEG q72-78, 250-400KB target
- Descriptive alt text on every image
- Lighthouse targets: ≥90 performance, ≥95 SEO / accessibility / best practices
- Phone as `tel:` link in E.164 format

Surfaced on the page as one plain line, not a technical section: *"Built properly — fast, works on a phone, and set up so Google can actually read it."*

## The handover skill (planned)

Idea: hand clients a **skill** at handover that walks them through the extras they might want later — Google Search Console, GA4, Bing Webmaster Tools, citations.

Frame as **available, not promised**. Craig's own read: most small businesses set analytics up and never look at it again. Including it as an optional extra adds perceived value without creating a support obligation.

## Open question — does the self-run promise hold at scale?

On a single page, "type what you want changed" is obviously true and easy to demo.

On a larger site with service pages, location pages and schema, "add a new service page" is a much bigger request — and if it comes out wrong or breaks the SEO structure, the core differentiator takes the damage.

Not a live problem while the offer is single-page only. Worth testing before ever selling a bigger build on the same promise.
