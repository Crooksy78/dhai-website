What Would You Lose If Your Go-To AI Person Left Today?

How to keep good AI work within business

Overview
  • See why AI work done on personal accounts walks out the door with the person
  • Find out what your team has already built with AI
  • Follow three steps to keep that work with the business
Free resource: a copy-and-paste prompt that turns someone's AI instructions into a guide anyone can follow

I was recently talking to a senior manager at a mid-sized business about their AI use. He mentioned a couple of things that were good examples of issues I see all the time.

He'd used Claude to create a pretty handy tool to deal with a very specific accounting issue. He also had an operations manager who'd created some nice automations to move product order info from one system to another.

In both cases, the systems had been created in personal AI accounts. And nothing had been written down anywhere.

As the manager himself put it: "No one knows where all this stuff is."

If either he or that operations manager leaves their role, the business will potentially lose that work. And the next guy will need to come in and create their own version from scratch.

Where Does This Work Actually Live?

Somewhere on your team there's probably someone who's worked out how to get AI to do a job properly. It might've taken some trial and error over days or weeks, but now it works great every time.

Unfortunately for many business owners, that know-how usually lives in the personal ChatGPT or Claude account of that staff member.

Or maybe just in their head.

If that staff member leaves, they've just lost the ability to automate that task. So the next staff member has to come in and start again.

Ethan Mollick, a Wharton professor who writes about AI at work, calls these people "secret cyborgs". They often keep quiet about their AI use because they're not sure it's allowed, or they're worried about what it means for their job.

So you may have no idea what's been built.

What Happens to Their AI Work When They're Away or Leave?

On a personal account, it stays with them. While they're on leave nobody else can get in, and when they leave it goes too. OpenAI's own help pages say a person's chats stay in their personal account unless they choose to move them into a company workspace.

A business account helps, but it doesn't solve everything. On ChatGPT Business, when someone is removed, their projects pass to a workspace owner, but their own chats and files don't, and the owner can't see them. On Claude's Team and Enterprise plans, the business's nominated owner manages the accounts and their data.

So whichever plan you're on, the fix is the same: get the good stuff written down somewhere the business owns.

How to Capture Your Team's AI Work in Three Steps

There's no one right way to do this. What matters is that you end up understanding how the work is actually being done, and that what your team has worked out stays with the business.

Step 1: Ask Three Questions

At your next staff meeting, ask everyone:

  • What do you use AI for at work?
  • Which account is it on: yours or the business's?
  • Is any of it written down anywhere?

Make it about finding and rewarding good ideas, not checking up on people. When someone's built something useful, thank them first. Tell them it's a great idea and you want the whole team to use it. Writing it down is how it becomes part of the business. You're not taking it off them.

Step 2: Get the AI to Write It Up

The person who built it opens the same chat or project they built it in and pastes this:

Turn what we built here into a step-by-step guide someone else in the business could follow without me. Include: what it does and why, when to use it, what you need before you start (logins, files, tools), each step in order, the exact instructions I give you word for word, what a good result looks like, common problems and how to fix them, and who checks the result before it's used.

Because it's the same chat, the AI can see how the thing was built, so it does most of the writing for them.

Then save the real pieces, not just a description: the exact instructions they type in, any files or templates they uploaded, and one or two examples of a good result. Those pieces are what actually do the work. Without them, nobody can rebuild it.

While they're at it, check what business or customer information has gone into their personal account. Anything sensitive should come out.

Step 3: Test It and File It

Have a different staff member follow the guide from scratch while the person who built it watches and stays quiet. Every spot where the tester gets stuck needs fixing in the guide.

Save it with your other procedures, in a shared drive or wherever you keep how-tos. Name the person responsible for it and set a date to check it still works, say every three months. AI tools change quickly.

Going one step further

Instead of a written guide or SOP, you can turn the process into a Skill. A Skill is a set of instructions, saved inside the AI. Anyone who has access to the Skill can then just run it whenever they need that job done.

It takes a bit more work to set up, but for a job your team does every week it can be the better move. On a business plan, the whole team can use the same Skill.

Should Your Go-To AI Person Be on a Business Account?

You don't need to move everyone onto a company plan on day one. But if someone in the business is clearly a bit of a power user, it's worth looking at putting them on a team account. That way the projects they build sit with the business, and you can still reach them if that person moves on.

Keep Your Team's AI Work With the Business

Your team is already building useful things with AI. That's a good thing, and it's worth encouraging. Just make sure what they build belongs to the business, so it's still there when someone's on holidays, off sick or moves on.

If you do one thing this week, start the conversation. Ask your team how they're using AI. You might be surprised by what they've already built.
